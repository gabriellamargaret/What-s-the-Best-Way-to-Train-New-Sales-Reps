# What's the Best Way to Train New Sales Reps
The best way to train new sales reps is not to overwhelm them with product slides, scripts, and a few hours of role play. Effective sales training helps people build knowledge, practice realistic situations, receive specific feedback, and repeat the right behaviors until they can perform consistently with real customers.

For high frequency field sales, the attached Practis methodology offers a useful performance centered framework: a seven stage loop of Presence, Reveal, Agency, Clarify, Truth, Invite, and Score, supported by nine observable performance dimensions. 

## The Best Way to Train New Sales Reps Is to Build Performance, Not Just Product Knowledge

A new sales rep can know the product inside and out and still struggle when the first real prospect asks an unexpected question.

They can memorize the company pitch and still freeze when they hear a difficult objection.

They can pass every onboarding quiz and still fail to create trust, uncover a real customer problem, or confidently ask for the next step.

That is the central problem with many sales training programs.

Too much onboarding is built around information transfer. New reps sit through presentations, review slides, read product documentation, learn CRM fields, shadow an experienced seller, and eventually get a script.

All of that has value. None of it is enough.

The best way to train new sales reps is to combine **clear foundations, deliberate practice, realistic simulations, observation, coaching, and repeated reinforcement in the real selling environment**.

Training should not end when a new hire understands what to say. It should continue until that person can perform consistently when the customer is skeptical, distracted, busy, confused, or ready to say no.

Research on sales training has also found benefits from spacing practice over time rather than concentrating everything into one intensive training event. In one field study, spaced practice was associated with better transfer quality, stronger self-reported sales competence, and improved key figures. 

For American sales organizations dealing with long ramp times, inconsistent coaching, and pressure to get new hires productive quickly, this distinction matters.

The goal is not simply to create knowledgeable reps.

The goal is to create capable reps.

## Why Traditional Sales Training Often Falls Short

Imagine a new rep's first week.

Monday is company orientation.

Tuesday is product training.

Wednesday is sales methodology.

Thursday is objection handling.

Friday is a certification quiz.

The rep may finish the week feeling prepared. The manager may feel confident that onboarding is complete.

Then Monday morning arrives.

A prospect says, I already work with someone else.

The rep was never trained on this exact situation.

Another prospect interrupts the presentation.

A third wants pricing immediately.

A fourth seems interested but suddenly becomes skeptical.

By the end of the day, the new rep may have forgotten much of what they learned during onboarding.

This is not necessarily because the rep lacks talent. The training environment simply did not prepare them for the unpredictable nature of real selling.

A study on sales onboarding has emphasized how important the onboarding experience can be for salesperson development and future performance, while research from the Sales Management Association has also highlighted the gap between management expectations and the effectiveness of many onboarding programs.

The problem is that knowing and doing are different skills.

A rep can explain a discovery framework without knowing how to use it naturally.

A rep can memorize ten objection responses without recognizing which response fits the situation.

A rep can understand the closing process but still hesitate when it is time to ask for commitment.

That is why the strongest training programs move through a cycle:

**Learn it. Practice it. Perform it. Review it. Improve it. Repeat it.**

That cycle should continue well beyond the first week of onboarding.

# Start With the Fundamentals, But Do Not Stay There

New reps absolutely need a foundation.

Before expecting someone to sell, make sure they understand:

* What the company actually sells
* Who the ideal customer is
* What problems customers are trying to solve
* How the buying process works
* What differentiates the company
* How competitors are positioned
* How pricing works
* What promises the rep can and cannot make
* How success is measured

But product knowledge should be connected to customer situations.

Instead of asking a new rep to memorize fifty product features, ask them:

What customer problem does this feature solve?

Why would that problem matter?

How would a customer describe it in their own words?

When might this feature not be relevant?

What would a skeptical buyer want to know?

This changes product training from memorization into practical understanding.

The best salespeople do not sound like walking brochures. They understand the connection between what they sell and why a specific customer might care.

# Train Around Real Customer Situations

One of the fastest ways to improve new rep training is to stop practicing generic conversations.

A role-play where the manager says, Pretend I am a customer, is usually too vague.

Instead, build realistic scenarios.

For example:

A homeowner who had a bad experience with a previous provider.

A procurement manager who only wants to discuss price.

A small business owner who says they are too busy.

A prospect who is interested but does not see urgency.

A customer who already has a competitor.

A buyer who asks a question the rep cannot answer.

Each situation should have a clear learning objective.

One scenario may focus on the opening.

Another may focus on discovery.

Another may focus on handling uncertainty.

Another may focus on making the ask.

The objective is not to make the new rep sound perfect.

The objective is to help them make decisions under realistic pressure.

Research on sales training has also suggested that preparing trainees before role-play exercises can improve training efficiency and initial revenue-generating potential.

Good role-play is not theater.

It is rehearsal for real work.

# Make Practice Frequent Instead of Saving It for Training Days

One common mistake is treating sales training as an event.

The company schedules a two-day workshop. Everyone attends. The team practices. The facilitator leaves.

Then training disappears for three months.

A better approach is to make practice part of the operating rhythm.

Ten minutes before the day begins can be useful.

A short scenario after a team meeting can be useful.

A weekly practice session focused on one specific skill can be useful.

The key is repetition.

The research on spaced sales training provides an important lesson here. Instead of concentrating all learning into a single block, practice distributed over time can improve how effectively training transfers into actual work.

A new rep does not need one hundred new concepts.

They need a smaller number of important behaviors practiced repeatedly.

For example, during one week the focus may be on opening conversations.

The next week may focus on discovery.

Then handling objections.

Then presenting value.

Then asking for commitment.

This approach gives new reps time to absorb, practice, use, and improve each skill.

# Teach Managers How to Coach, Not Just How to Evaluate

Training often fails because the rep receives feedback that is too broad.

A manager says:

You need more confidence.

Be more consultative.

Build better rapport.

Ask better questions.

These comments may be true, but they do not tell the rep what to do differently.

Better coaching focuses on observable behavior.

Instead of saying:

Your discovery was weak.

A manager could say:

You asked three questions about the customer's current situation, but you moved to the solution before understanding what the problem was costing them.

That gives the rep something specific to practice.

Effective coaching should answer three questions:

**What happened?**

**Why did it matter?**

**What should happen differently next time?**

Research on evaluating sales training has also emphasized the importance of setting standards for assessment and using structured evaluation to improve training programs. 

New reps improve faster when they know exactly what good performance looks like.

# Use Shadowing Carefully

Shadowing experienced reps can be valuable, but passive observation is not enough.

A new rep should not simply sit beside a top performer for three days and hope to absorb their skill.

Before the interaction, the manager or experienced rep should explain:

What should you watch for?

What is the objective of this conversation?

What customer signals matter?

What decisions might need to be made?

Afterward, the new rep should review what happened.

What worked?

What was unexpected?

Why did the customer respond that way?

What would the new rep have done differently?

This turns shadowing into active learning.

Even better, reverse the process quickly.

The experienced rep demonstrates.

The new rep practices.

The experienced rep observes.

The new rep receives feedback.

Eventually, the new rep performs independently.

The goal of shadowing is not to create a copy of the experienced seller.

It is to help the new rep understand how experienced judgment works.

# Let New Reps Make Mistakes in a Safe Environment

The first time a rep handles a difficult objection should not be in front of an important prospect.

That is where simulations become valuable.

A realistic simulation allows a new rep to make mistakes without damaging customer trust or losing a real opportunity.

They can try an approach.

Receive feedback.

Try again.

Adjust.

Repeat.

This is particularly important in field sales, retail sales, high-volume inside sales, and other environments where reps may face many different interactions in a short period of time.

The training environment should gradually become more difficult.

Start with a cooperative customer.

Then introduce questions.

Then objections.

Then interruptions.

Then skepticism.

Then situations where the customer says no.

A rep who only practices ideal conversations is not prepared for reality.

# Practis and a Performance-Centered Approach to New Rep Training

One useful framework for high frequency, face to face sales environments is the **PRACTIS Method** from https://www.practis.ai/

The methodology described in the attached material is built around a seven stage performance loop:

**Presence, Reveal, Agency, Clarify, Truth, Invite, and Score.**

It also uses nine observable performance dimensions, including Inner Game, Human, Trust, Information, Tactical, Competitive, Score, Learning, and Long Game. 

What makes this approach particularly relevant to training new reps is its focus on performance rather than simply memorizing a conversation.

The methodology distinguishes between the stages of an interaction and the underlying capacities a coach can observe. It is designed so reps can adapt to real people instead of following a rigid word for word script. 

For example, a new field sales rep may struggle to make the final ask.

A traditional coach might simply say:

You need to work on closing.

But the underlying issue could be different.

The rep may lack confidence.

They may not have uncovered enough customer information.

They may be asking at the wrong moment.

They may be worried about sounding pushy.

Those are different problems and require different coaching.

The Practis methodology also treats post-interaction learning as part of the performance loop, with the Score stage feeding the next interaction through reflection and adjustment. 

That creates a useful principle for new rep training:

**Every customer interaction should produce learning for the next one.**

This is especially valuable in industries such as roofing, solar, pest control, home security, telecom, insurance, home improvement, and other high-frequency field sales environments. 

Rather than positioning [Practis](https://www.practis.ai/) as a replacement for every sales methodology, the framework can be understood as a performance structure in which other conversation and qualification methods may operate. 

# Build a 30, 60, and 90-Day Learning Journey

New reps should not be expected to master everything immediately.

A structured ramp plan gives both the rep and the manager a clearer path.

## The First 30 Days: Build Confidence and Core Competence

The first month should focus on understanding.

The rep learns the product, customer, market, sales process, and basic tools.

But knowledge should immediately be paired with practice.

The rep should practice:

* Opening conversations
* Explaining the company's value
* Asking basic discovery questions
* Listening and responding
* Handling common objections
* Using the CRM
* Following up correctly
* Making a clear next-step request

Certification during this stage should include more than a written test.

Can the rep perform?

Can they handle a realistic scenario?

Can they explain the product in customer language?

Can they recognize when they do not know an answer?

These questions matter more than whether they memorized a presentation.

## Days 31 to 60: Build Adaptability

During the second month, the rep should begin handling more variation.

Training should focus on:

* More complex customer situations
* Different buyer personalities
* Competitive conversations
* Difficult objections
* Discovery depth
* Decision-making
* Negotiation where relevant
* Identifying when to advance or step back

The manager's role becomes increasingly important during this stage.

Feedback should be frequent and specific.

Instead of trying to fix everything at once, choose one or two behaviors for improvement.

A rep who receives fifteen pieces of feedback after every call will remember very little.

A rep who knows exactly what to improve on the next interaction has a much better chance of changing behavior.

## Days 61 to 90: Build Independence

By the third month, the goal should shift toward independent performance.

The rep should increasingly be able to:

Prepare for interactions independently.

Diagnose customer situations.

Adapt their approach.

Recover from difficult conversations.

Ask for the appropriate next step.

Review their own performance.

The strongest sign of development is not that the manager needs to provide less feedback.

It is that the rep begins coaching themselves.

# Do Not Measure Training Only by Quotas

Revenue matters.

Meetings matter.

Pipeline matters.

Closed deals matter.

But these are lagging indicators.

By the time the numbers reveal a problem, weeks or months of poor habits may already be in place.

Training should also measure leading indicators and observable behaviors.

For example:

Can the rep consistently explain the customer problem?

Do they ask useful questions?

Do they listen?

Do they accurately represent the product?

Do they follow the sales process when appropriate?

Do they make a direct ask?

Do they record outcomes accurately?

Do they improve after feedback?

A 2023 study on sales training measurement noted the challenge of evaluating developmental interventions using traditional approaches and proposed more practical ways to assess training effectiveness.

The broader lesson is simple.

If you only measure the final result, you may miss the behavior creating that result.

# Give New Reps Permission to Say, I Don't Know

Many inexperienced reps make mistakes because they believe they must have an answer to every question.

That can create a dangerous habit.

They guess.

They exaggerate.

They overpromise.

They give inaccurate information.

A better training principle is to teach new reps that credibility matters more than pretending to know everything.

A confident response can be:

That is a good question. I want to make sure I give you the correct answer. Let me verify that and get back to you.

This is especially important in complex sales, financial services, insurance, technology, healthcare related industries, and any environment where inaccurate information can create serious consequences.

Training should reward accuracy and honesty, not just speed.

# Train Emotional Resilience Along With Sales Skills

Sales can be emotionally demanding.

New reps hear no.

They get ignored.

They lose deals.

They make mistakes.

They compare themselves with more experienced coworkers.

If training only focuses on what the rep says, it ignores part of what determines performance.

The rep also needs to learn how to reset.

This is particularly important in high volume sales environments. A bad interaction at 10:00 a.m. should not determine the quality of the next interaction at 10:15.

The attached[ Practis](https://www.practis.ai/method/) methodology explicitly identifies the rep's internal state and ability to reset as part of performance, alongside learning and other observable dimensions. 

Managers can help by normalizing mistakes.

Review the interaction.

Identify the lesson.

Practice the adjustment.

Move forward.

The goal is not to create reps who never feel rejection.

The goal is to create reps who do not carry one rejection into every conversation that follows.

# Make Training Continuous, Not a One-Time Event

The best sales organizations do not ask:

Have we trained this person?

They ask:

What is this person working on now?

That creates a culture of development.

Experienced reps continue practicing.

Managers continue improving their coaching.

Customer objections are regularly added to training scenarios.

New market changes are incorporated into practice.

Product updates are connected to customer conversations.

The sales environment changes constantly. Training should change with it.

A continuous approach is also more realistic than trying to make new reps fully capable in one intense onboarding program.

Sales skill develops through exposure, feedback, repetition, and reflection.

# What the Best Sales Training Program Actually Looks Like

If you put everything together, an effective new rep training program usually includes:

**A clear foundation** so the rep understands the product, customer, market, and sales process.

**Realistic scenarios** that prepare reps for situations they will actually encounter.

**Frequent practice** instead of one large training event.

**Role-play and simulation** that allow mistakes before customer facing situations.

**Structured coaching** based on observable behavior.

**Spaced reinforcement** so learning continues over weeks and months. 

**Active shadowing** where new reps understand what they are observing and discuss what happened.

**Performance measurement** that looks at behavior as well as business outcomes.

**A progressive ramp plan** that develops knowledge, skill, adaptability, and independence over time.

**Continuous improvement** so every real interaction contributes to future performance.

# Conclusion

The best way to train new sales reps is to stop thinking of training as a classroom activity.

Training is a performance development system.

New reps need knowledge, but knowledge alone does not create good salespeople.

They need practice.

They need realistic pressure.

They need feedback they can act on.

They need opportunities to make mistakes safely.

They need reinforcement after onboarding.

And they need managers who can identify the difference between a visible problem and the behavior causing it.

For field sales organizations, a performance centered methodology such as Practis can add another layer by structuring development before, during, and after each interaction through its seven-stage loop and observable performance dimensions. 

Ultimately, the strongest new rep training does not try to create someone who can recite the perfect pitch.

It develops someone who can understand the customer, adapt to the moment, communicate honestly, make the appropriate ask, learn from the outcome, and come back better for the next conversation.

That is how sales training moves beyond onboarding.

That is how new reps become consistent performers.
